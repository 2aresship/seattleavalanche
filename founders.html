Founders

Every newsroom starts with somebody. This one started with me.

Sandra Kellen
Founder
At a glance
  • Hometown: Kaua'i, Hawai'i
  • Career: Eleven years at Cloud 9 Store 1217, St. Louis. Sales associate, then Assistant Manager of online fulfillment.
  • Memory: Highly superior autobiographical. Exactly as useful as it sounds.
  • Now: Seattle. Founder of The Avalanche.

Hi. I'm Sandra.

A few words from the person who started this thing.

I spent eleven years working at a big-box store in St. Louis called Cloud 9, Store 1217. Most of that time I was a sales associate. In my last year I made Assistant Manager of the online fulfillment center, which is a very long title for a job that was mostly me and a headset. Before St. Louis there was Hawai'i, where I grew up on Kaua'i. People assume moving from an island to Missouri is a downgrade. St. Louis has its charms. One of them is that you are never more than four minutes from a store exactly like the one you work in.

I am not the person anyone expects to found a newsroom. I know how I come across. I'm quiet. I say yes when people ask me for things, even unreasonable things, especially unreasonable things. I have trouble bringing up legitimate concerns in meetings, and when I do manage it, someone talks over me and the moment passes. For years I compensated by telling stories about myself that were, let's say, embellished. If you have ever invented a boyfriend to seem more interesting in a break room, you already understand me better than most of my managers did.

Here is the thing they never noticed about me: I remember everything. Not in a poetic way. Clinically. It's called highly superior autobiographical memory, and it means I can tell you what happened on any Tuesday you name, including what the break room smelled like and exactly which manager told me to be quiet in it. My memory is the single most valuable reporting tool I own, and I got it the way you get anything worthwhile at a big-box store: by enduring.

The last straw was not dramatic. Retail life is never dramatic, that is the whole problem. It was a coin toss. An actual coin toss, in that break room, over a man named Jerry, which I lost. Then won back. Then lost again. Around the third flip I looked at the ceiling tiles and thought: somewhere out there is a job where the important decisions are not settled this way. It took two more years, a wedding, and a coworker named Carol attempting to sabotage it in ways I still cannot discuss calmly, before I moved to Seattle and started The Avalanche.

So here is the philosophy, and now you know where it comes from. This newsroom has no bylines, because I spent a decade being the person nobody credited and I refuse to pass that on. It publishes slowly, because nobody ever listened to me in a hurry and the stories that mattered took time to confirm anyway. There are no managers, because I had Dina. If you never worked under someone who told you, verbatim, "I'd like to meet your mother and shove you back up inside her," then this newsroom will feel like a foreign film to you: subtitled, a little intense, not for everyone. If you have, it will feel like home.

What I want The Avalanche to be is simple. A small paper that does one thing properly: checks everything, prints carefully, protects whoever talks to it, and answers its mail. I spent eleven years being asked to smile while the store fell apart around me. This is me not smiling. This is me checking the receipts instead.

You can reach me through the drop box. I read everything myself, usually late, always eventually. That is not a slogan. It is just what happens when the whole staff is one person who remembers every single day.

— Sandra
