City Hall Quietly Expands Surveillance Network Across Capitol Hill

Internal procurement records show 214 new surveillance cameras approved in a program never announced to the council committee that oversees it, and a private analytics contract buried in a routine consent agenda.

By An anonymous correspondent · August 21, 2026 · 14 min read

The cameras arrived the way most things arrive in city government: quietly, in batches, inside documents almost nobody reads. Between June and July of this year, according to procurement records obtained by this publication, the city approved the purchase and installation of 214 new fixed-position surveillance cameras across Capitol Hill, a figure that does not appear in any public budget document, briefing, or council presentation.

The program, referred to internally as “Comprehensive Corridor Safety Modernization”, was funded through a combination of a state safety grant and a reallocation from the transportation capital fund, a maneuver that kept it out of the general budget, and out of sight of the council committee formally responsible for overseeing surveillance technology.

What the records show

The ledger lists three purchase orders totaling just over $2.1 million. Each order names the same vendor; two were signed by an office that, per the city’s own surveillance ordinance, has no authority to approve imaging equipment. The third order includes a line item for “analytics integration services”, a contract awarded without competitive bidding under an emergency exemption that the ordinance reserves for imminent threats to public safety.

“Nothing about this program is an emergency. An emergency is what you call when you don’t want anyone asking questions.” , a city employee familiar with the procurement file

Twelve camera locations overlap with the routes of three annual protest marches. City officials have previously testified, under oath, in a different proceeding, that fixed cameras are not used to monitor demonstrations. The new placement map, embedded in one of the purchase orders, makes no mention of that policy.

The analytics clause

More consequential than the cameras may be the software attached to them. The “analytics integration” contract commits the city to a three-year term with automatic renewal, and its technical exhibits reference capabilities, object classification, dwell-time analysis, “pattern-of-life” flagging, that go well beyond recording.

Asked about the contract, the vendor’s account manager did not dispute the exhibit descriptions. The mayor’s office declined to comment. The chair of the oversight committee, reached Thursday, said they had “never seen” the program and would be “asking very direct questions this week.”

What we don’t know yet

  • Whether any footage has already been shared with other agencies.
  • Who signed the emergency exemption, and on whose advice.
  • Whether the analytics system has ever been tested on stored protest footage.

This story will be updated as those questions are answered. If you work inside City Hall and recognize any of this, the drop box is open, and it doesn’t know who you are.
